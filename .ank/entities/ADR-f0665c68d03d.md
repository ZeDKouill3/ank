## Why

The reader's entities panel lists every kind side by side: tasks, decisions,
specifications and log entries in one column. Every identifier was yellow
whatever it named, so the only way to tell a `TASK-` row from an `ADR-` row was
to read its prefix. On a corpus of fifteen hundred entities, where most rows are
tasks, finding the handful of decisions meant reading every row. That is the job
colour does well, and the one the palette forbade it.

The same listing drew `done` and `accepted` alike, in green, and `closed` and
`superseded` alike, dim. Finished work is most of an old corpus and drew the eye
as much as anything live; a closed task, work that will not be done, receded
exactly as far as a document that was replaced and lives on in its successor.

The palette was chosen on a preview build of the reader, by the person who reads
it, over three rounds: an ADR in magenta read as `[proposed]` on the same row; a
vivid orange outshone the tasks around it; index 173 did neither.

## Why a decision, and a supersession behind it

SPEC-89070ce7f3b8 is accepted and bounds the palette "here rather than in the
code", so its rows cannot move by a change to `meaning.rs` alone. The table
anticipated this in its own words: a surface wanting `ADR-` to read differently
from `TASK-` has to change the rows of the table, not add a call site nobody
reviews. This decision is that change, made where it is reviewed.

An ADR cannot amend a specification, so the palette moves by superseding the
document that carries it, proposed in the same change as the code so that the
two are ratified together.

ADR-1f70ce2c3eac is untouched. It requires one table and allows one renderer per
surface, and both still hold: what changes is what the table says, not where it
lives or who reads it.

## The rows, and why these

- **task: yellow.** Unchanged. Tasks are most of any corpus, and keeping them
  where they were means most of what a person already learned does not move.
- **adr: index 173, a soft orange.** All eight base colours are spent by a
  status or a kind, so this is the one rung off them. It is a fixed colour
  rather than one of the terminal's own, so a theme does not retune it; the
  terminal reader's guard, which admitted only the sixteen, now names this one
  index and still refuses every other and every background.
- **spec: blue.** Rare in a listing, and reference material nobody claims.
- **log: dim.** A log entry records what happened and asks for nothing.
- **done, finished: grey.** Finished business recedes. Bright black rather than
  dim, so it stays apart from a superseded document.
- **accepted: green.** A ratified decision binds every change after it; it is
  not finished business, and it no longer shares a role with `done`.
- **closed: red.** Work that will not be done is what a person scanning the list
  is asked to see first. The table gives it a role of its own, `Dropped`, and
  not `Fault`: nothing in the corpus is wrong, and the two only happen to share
  a colour on these surfaces. The earlier rule, "dim and never red, nothing here
  failed", argued that painting a close as an error would teach an agent to
  avoid the honest move. What an agent reads is never styled, so the argument
  binds the person at the terminal and not the agent; that person asked for it.

The identifier and the status never share a column, so an orange `ADR-` beside a
magenta `[proposed]`, or a yellow `TASK-` beside a red `[closed]`, are told
apart by where they stand.

## What it costs

`Role::Identifier` leaves the shared table, so `ank-cli`'s `style.rs` and
`ank-tui`'s `paint.rs` stopped compiling until each rendered the new roles,
which is the intended effect of a total match on `Role`. The CLI's listings
(`ank find`, `ank context`, `ank show`) change colour too, deliberately: one
table means the CLI and the reader agree that a decision looks like a decision.
